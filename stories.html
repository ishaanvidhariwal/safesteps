Inspiring stories of women, for women, by women

Stories of what girls and women are told to put up with, and the courage it takes to say it out loud. Real accounts appear here only with the contributor's permission.

About these stories. The first is the founder's own account. Every other story here is illustrative: the names are made up and they are not real people. They are written to reflect experiences that many girls and women in India describe. Some describe harassment and violence. Real stories from our community will be added with permission. Click a card to read it.

“It was never about the window”

Ishaanvi Dhariwal, founder. Two men on a motorbike, a school bus, and what I was told afterwards.

It started in 2022, on the bus ride home from school. Two men on motorbikes began chasing the bus, and then followed me all the way to my house, recording me with their phones. Inside the bus, the other children laughed and mocked me. They asked me to pose for the camera.

I panicked and called my mother. She told me this was common, that I needed to be strong, not panic, and give it back to them. My father blamed me instead: “You must have been looking outside the window.”

SafeSteps is what I built because no girl should be told that this is normal, or that it is her fault.

Founder's story · 2022

“Everyone watched. Nobody stopped them.”

Shanti. My daughter ran away so she would not be married, and I was beaten for it.

My daughter was sixteen. Her marriage had been fixed to a man almost twice her age, and she did not want it. She told me, and I did not know what to say, because no one had ever asked me what I wanted.

One night she left. In the morning my husband and his father pulled me out into the lane and beat me, in front of the whole neighbourhood, asking where she had gone. I did not know. People came out to watch. Some looked away. Nobody stepped in.

I still do not know where my daughter is. I am not angry with her. Every day I hope she is somewhere with a book in her hand, where no one is deciding her life for her.

— Shanti

“Nobody asked why my marks fell”

Preeti. I stopped raising my hand in class so nobody would look at me.

In class nine, a boy from the senior class followed me to the gate every day and passed comments. I told my mother. She said, “Ignore it. If you make noise, people will talk, and who will marry you?”

So I stopped speaking. Then I stopped raising my hand in class, because I did not want anyone looking at me. My marks fell. Nobody asked why.

I am telling you this so that some other girl knows it was never because she was too much.

— Preeti

“I draw trees on the back of old papers”

Titu. My brother goes to school. I stay home.

My brother goes to school. I stay home, because the road to my school has men who stare, and my mother says staying home is for my safety.

I help her with the cooking. I finish my chores early, and then I draw trees on the back of old papers. I like drawing the roots best, because roots do not have to go anywhere.

I wonder why it is always me who stays. Nobody has told the men to stay home.

— Titu

“I took the long way home for two years”

Meena. There is a shortcut to my college through a narrow lane.

There is a shortcut to my college through a narrow lane. A group of boys sat there every afternoon and called out names. I started taking the long road, forty minutes longer, and told my mother the bus was late.

I got used to being tired. One day I counted the hours I had given them: more than three hundred. Then I told my friends. Now four of us walk the shortcut together, and the boys have gone quiet.

I still wish I had not needed a group to walk on a public road.

— Meena

“Wash it quietly, before anyone sees”

Kavita. I was twelve when I got my period at school.

I was twelve when I got my period at school. There was a stain on my skirt, and a boy in the back row said it out loud. My teacher gave me her scarf and sent me home.

My mother told me to wash it quietly, away from my father and brother, and never to speak of it. For years I thought something was wrong with me.

I am fifteen now. I keep pads in my bag, and I tell the younger girls where they are.

— Kavita

Illustrative · Body and health

“Nobody asked what he had said”

Sunita. I work at a cloth shop.

I work at a cloth shop. The owner's son stood too close and said things when his father was away. When I told the manager, he said the boy was young and I should be grateful for the job.

I stayed for a year. I left the day I found another job, and no one asked me why.

I am saying it now so it is on record: it was never my job to put up with it.

— Sunita

Illustrative · Work

“Why were you sitting like that?”

Pooja. A boy from tuition took a photo of me without asking and posted it in a group with a joke under it.

A boy from tuition took a photo of me without asking and posted it in a group with a joke under it. By evening half my school had seen it.

My parents were angrier at me than at him. “Why were you sitting like that?” I had been sitting at a desk.

A teacher I trusted helped me report it, and the group was removed. I still wonder why the first question is always about the girl.

— Pooja

Illustrative · Online

“My brother can go out after dark”

Rukhsar. I am nineteen and I have a curfew of six.

I am nineteen and I have a curfew of six. My brother is fifteen and has none. When I asked why, my mother said, “The world is not safe for girls.” I asked, “Then why do we fix the girls?”

She had no answer then. She is thinking about it now. Last week I stayed at a college event until eight, and she waited at the gate herself.

It is a small step. I am counting it as a win.

— Rukhsar

Illustrative · Home

“Someone did it for me once”

Anjali. A man on the bus kept pressing against me.

A man on the bus kept pressing against me. I froze. A woman sitting nearby stood up, looked straight at him, and said loudly, “Sir, move.” Other people turned to look. He got off at the next stop.

I thanked her. She said, “Someone did it for me once.”

In every crowded bus now, I try to be that woman for somebody else.

— Anjali

Illustrative · Public transport

“I will answer for my daughter”

Nisha. I have an offer to study in another city.

I have an offer to study in another city. My grandmother says girls in our family do not go away to study. The aunties came to our house and asked, “What will people say?”

My father sat quiet all evening. The next morning he told them, “I will answer for my daughter.”

I do not know yet if I will be allowed to go. But it was the first time I heard my father say my name like that.

— Nisha

Illustrative · Education

“They told me to try again for a boy”

Rani. After my second daughter was born, my mother-in-law did not speak to me for a month.

After my second daughter was born, my mother-in-law did not speak to me for a month. Visitors came to say, “Next time, a boy.”

I looked at my girls sleeping and thought: they will be told all their lives that they are a burden. I decided they would not believe it.

My elder one is in third class and wants to be a doctor. I tell her she can, every night, before the lamp goes off.

— Rani

Illustrative · Family

“He gave the bicycle back”

Gudiya. I used to cycle to school.

I used to cycle to school. After a boy followed me one evening, my father hid my bicycle. “It is for your safety,” he said. I walked in the heat for a whole year.

This summer my teacher spoke to him. He gave it back, and said sorry for the first time in his life. He still worries.

But now I ride in the morning with two girls from our lane, and the road feels like ours.

— Gudiya

Illustrative · Home

“Do not carry it alone”

Sarika. Someone made a fake account with my name and sent cruel messages to my friends, written to sound like me.

Someone made a fake account with my name and sent cruel messages to my friends, written to sound like me. I felt like I was disappearing.

My cousin helped me take screenshots. We reported the account on the platform and to the cyber cell. It took three weeks, and then the account was gone.

What I learned: write everything down, tell someone early, and do not carry it alone.

— Sarika

Illustrative · Online

“He said it was just a joke”

Mehak. A senior at my coaching centre messaged me every night and called it a joke.

A senior at my coaching centre messaged me every night and called it a joke. When I told him to stop, he said I was too sensitive. I told no one for two months because I was ashamed.

When I finally told the coordinator, she listened and said, “You did nothing wrong.” He was removed from the batch. Three other girls then said it had happened to them too.

We should not have needed to speak up one at a time.

— Mehak

Illustrative · School and coaching

“This is my passenger”

Jyoti. Late one night my auto broke down on a dark road.

Late one night my auto broke down on a dark road. Two men on a bike slowed down beside me. The auto driver, a man old enough to be my father, stepped in front of me and said, “This is my passenger.” They rode away.

He drove me home and would not take extra money.

I think about him whenever I hear that men cannot be trusted. Some can. We should ask more of the rest.

— Jyoti
